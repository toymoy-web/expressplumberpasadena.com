Buying an Older Home in Pasadena? Check the Plumbing First

By the Pasadena Express Plumbing team · Published July 25, 2025

A general home inspection covers plumbing at a surface level. Older homes near the historic core deserve a closer look.

A standard home inspection checks that fixtures work and looks for obvious leaks, but it usually doesn't dig into pipe material, age, or the kind of slow-developing issues that matter most in a home built decades ago. If you're buying an older home in Pasadena's historic neighborhoods, it's worth going beyond the standard inspection on plumbing specifically.

None of this is meant to scare anyone off an older home: plenty of them have perfectly serviceable plumbing. It's about knowing what you're actually buying before you own it, not after.

It's also worth remembering that plumbing age isn't the only factor: the specific neighborhood matters too. Homes closer to Pasadena's historic downtown core tend to be older on average than those further out toward the Harris County line, so the same build-year cutoff doesn't apply evenly across every part of the city.

Find Out What the Supply Lines Are Made Of

Homes built before the 1970s in Pasadena often still have original galvanized steel supply lines, which have a 40-to-60-year service life and may already be near or past that window. A magnet test on any exposed pipe (basement, crawlspace, under a sink) is a quick way to check. It sticks to galvanized steel but not to copper or PEX.

If the lines are confirmed galvanized and the home is on the older end of that age range, factor a potential repipe into your real cost of ownership, even if nothing's actively failing yet.

Ask About the Sewer Lateral Specifically

The sewer line running from the house to the street is easy to overlook because it's completely out of sight, but older Pasadena homes sometimes still have original clay or Orangeburg pipe, both of which deteriorate structurally regardless of maintenance.

A camera inspection of the sewer lateral before closing is a genuinely worthwhile expense on an older home. It's the one part of the plumbing system that's expensive to fix and completely invisible until it fails.

Check the Water Heater's Real Age

The manufacture date is usually encoded in the serial number, not necessarily obvious at a glance: worth asking the inspector or seller directly rather than assuming a working unit is recent.

A poorly maintained tank can fail well before its rated lifespan, and sediment buildup is a common reason, so knowing the actual age matters.

A Quick Pre-Closing Checklist

None of this requires specialized knowledge on your part. It's mostly about asking the right questions and having the right inspections done before you're committed.

  • ✓Test any exposed supply pipe with a magnet to check for galvanized steel
  • ✓Ask for a sewer lateral camera inspection, especially on homes built before 1980
  • ✓Find the water heater's actual manufacture date, not just "looks newer"
  • ✓Ask whether the home has ever had a slab leak or foundation repair
  • ✓Check water pressure at multiple fixtures, not just the one the seller shows you

Getting a Second Opinion Before You're Locked In

If the standard home inspection raises any plumbing question at all, even a minor one, it's reasonable to request a follow-up visit from a plumber specifically, while you're still within your option period and before you're financially committed.

This costs less than most buyers expect relative to the price of discovering a major plumbing issue after closing, when your only options are far more expensive.

Budgeting for What You Find

If an inspection turns up galvanized pipe or an aging sewer lateral, that doesn't necessarily mean walking away from the purchase. It means factoring a realistic repipe or sewer replacement cost into your budget, either through price negotiation or planning for the work after you move in.

Knowing the number before you close gives you real options. Discovering it after closing leaves you with just one.
